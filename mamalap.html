The problem

Lack of support makes breastfeeding harder.

Without adequate support, mothers adjust their posture and rely on their arms to hold the baby, causing discomfort, awkward positioning, and latching difficulties — affecting the comfort and intimacy of breastfeeding.

The question

How might we make feeds on the move comfortable for both mother and baby?

Understanding the User

Observing feeding behaviours and identifying physical discomfort.

Exploring Support

Exploring different ways to support the mother and baby during feeding.

Prototyping & Testing

Building and testing foam prototypes to refine comfort, positioning and form.

Design goals

Reduce strain

Relieve the load on the back, shoulders and arms.

Improve positioning

Help maintain a natural position for both mother and baby.

Enable mobility

Make feeding easier in semi-public and travel settings.

Keep it simple

Avoid complicated straps, adjustments or setup.

The solution

MamaLap – a support system designed around the lap.

Natural positioning

Supports a more upright feeding posture, and can be put in place while already holding the baby.

Arm relief

Supports the baby’s weight, while the mother still feels she is holding them.

Detachable headrest

A base cushion and a detachable headrest, for height adjustment and more flexibility in positioning.

MamaLap carries the baby’s weight from underneath. Nothing goes between mother and baby — no straps, no fabric — so the closeness of the feed stays as it was.

How it works

01Position

Place the lap cushion on your lap and position the baby comfortably.

02Adjust

Add or reposition the headrest to adjust the height and alignment.

03Ergonomic contours

Contoured surfaces support the baby, guide hand placement, and leave room for the mother’s hands.

Understanding existing products and everyday solutions

I looked at existing breastfeeding products, their forms and functions, as well as the informal ways mothers adapt clothing and everyday objects for feeding and carrying.

Feeding sequence

  1. She holds the baby on her lap.

  2. She adjusts her kurti for access while arranging a shawl or towel for privacy.

  3. She carefully draws the baby closer, positioning them towards the breast to latch.

  4. After feeding, she gently moves the baby from her breast to her lap.

  5. She readjusts the front of her kurti to close it.

  6. She lifts the baby against her shoulder to burp.

Understanding through experience

To empathise with mothers, we recreated the experience of holding a baby while doing everyday tasks.

Simulation

  • 5 kg weighted bottle wrapped in a blanket
  • Held for 10 minutes in a feeding position
  • Everyday tasks done while holding it

Key findings

  • Frequent repositioning to relieve discomfort
  • The lap became a natural resting point
  • Two-handed tasks became difficult
  • Forward leaning

Quick mockups for weight distribution

Before detailed sketching, I made quick, low-fidelity mockups to try different ways of spreading the baby’s weight. They let me check wearability, ease of use, positioning, access and setup time early, before committing to a direction.

01Shoulder sling

  • Tested shoulder-based weight distribution.
  • Bulky and difficult to wear.

02Hip belt + arm cushion

  • Tested quick wearing and weight distribution.
  • Took too long to set up; the arm cushion lacked stability.
  • Very restrictive.

03Lap system

  • Explored a two-part, lap-based system.
  • The lap proved the most intuitive and accessible.
  • Two-part form kept; rotation removed.

Why the lap

While observing feeds, I noticed many mothers sat cross-legged. Crossing the legs lifts the lap and brings the baby’s head up towards the breast. They weren’t trying to raise the whole baby, only the head.

Prototyping and testing

01Prototype 1Carved foam

Test

  • Carved Duroflex foam, testing density and thigh curvature — checked with classmates across the 5th–95th percentile Indian female sitting thigh-thickness range.

Problems found

  • Slope too steep; unwanted curves.
  • Headrest shape ineffective.
  • Poor hand access.

Learnings for redesign

  • Gentler slope, refined curves, better headrest, more hand clearance.

02Prototype 2Fitted working model

Focus

  • A user-fitted, ergonomic working model for the 5th–95th percentile mother.

Key upgrades

  • Tailored stomach and thigh curves, added hand grooves, a Lycra cover and Velcro attachments.

Result

  • Working prototype developed; moved on to testing with babies.

03TestingWith mothers and babies

User testing insights

  • Hand placement wasn’t done the way I intended.
  • The inclination can be reduced.
  • More grooves are needed so hands can slide easily.

Final prototype

  • Base 25.6 × 18.2 cm fits narrow laps (32 cm hip breadth) and keeps knees free
  • Headrest 14.9 cm wide supports the baby’s head from birth to 6 months
  • Headrest 5.4 cm high tilts the baby towards the breast without lifting the body
