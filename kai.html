Key insights

City unfamiliarity

Visual distraction

Physical interaction

Many delivery riders are migrants. Riders new to a city may not yet have a mental map of its roads and landmarks.

Map clutter and poor screen visibility make it harder to pick out the information needed at a glance.

Tapping, zooming and switching between apps mean riders have to keep operating the phone while navigating.

Navigation uncertainty

Audio limitations

Work pressure

Riders need to interpret upcoming manoeuvres and confirm they are following the right route.

Some riders use one earbud or phone vibration to catch turn cues when spoken directions are difficult to hear.

Delivery targets and ratings create pressure to complete orders quickly.

Design direction

The research pointed to two areas to focus on: reducing the need to interact with the phone and making navigation easier to follow.

01 / Reduce manual interaction

02 / Simplify navigation information

Limit the need to tap, swipe, zoom and switch between apps while navigating. Use voice guidance to communicate key instructions when they’re needed.

Make the next manoeuvre easier to identify by prioritising relevant route information, useful landmarks and a clearer map.

Context-aware map zoom

The map adjusts its zoom based on the rider’s situation, showing the wider route when they need an overview and bringing the next turn into view when they’re on long stretches of road.

Before the ride — Route overview

During the ride — Upcoming turn

The map starts zoomed out so riders can see the route and build the mental model of the map. Once movement is detected, it zooms in for closer navigation.

On long, straight roads, the map zooms out to bring the next turn into view, helping riders anticipate where the route changes without manually adjusting the map.

What I learned

Riders’ workarounds can point to simple design changes that make a big difference.

The right design choice depends on the situation. What works in one context may not work in another.
